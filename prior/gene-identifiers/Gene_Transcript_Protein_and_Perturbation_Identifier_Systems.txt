       A first-principles monograph for the MANTRA perturbation-response data stack

                                                Abstract
Biological data commonly place gene symbols, gene-record accessions, transcript accessions, protein
accessions, guide identities, assay accessions, and model-coordinate keys beside one another. These
strings are related, but they identify different kinds of objects. This monograph develops the relevant
molecular biology from first principles, explains how genome annotations and biological databases are
constructed, gives a detailed account of single- and dual-guide CRISPR perturbation designs, and then
specifies the identifier architecture used by the MANTRA perturbation-response pipeline. The central
design principle is that biological identity is a typed, release-aware record with several orthogonal
projections rather than one universal name.

1      The Biological Identity Problem
1.1    Why a gene-identification system becomes difficult in practice
A software system often begins with an apparently simple requirement: every perturbagen and every
measured gene should have a stable key. The requirement becomes difficult as soon as data are
integrated across experimental generations and biological resources. A perturbation library may name a
target with the gene symbol that was current when the library was designed. A contemporary
nomenclature database may now use a different approved symbol. The raw single-cell file may also carry
an Ensembl gene identifier and a transcript identifier. A protein-derived prior may require a UniProt
accession. A chromatin-derived prior may originate from an ENCODE file interpreted against a GENCODE
annotation on a particular genome assembly. The prediction tensor may finally use a project-local
coordinate that was created to preserve duplicate display symbols.

All of these identifiers can describe one connected biological case. They still have different semantics. The
guide-pair identifier names an experimental construct. The HGNC ID names a human gene-nomenclature
record. The Ensembl transcript accession names one annotated transcript feature. The UniProt accession
names a protein knowledgebase entry. The ENCODE accession names an assay or data file. The MANTRA
resolved_gene_key names one model coordinate. A robust system preserves those identities as related
fields instead of compressing them into a single string.

The practical source of confusion is that the word gene is used at several levels. It can mean a biological
locus, a database record about that locus, a human-readable symbol, a row in a perturbation split, a
column in an expression matrix, or the intended target of a guide construct. The surrounding context
usually disambiguates the meaning in ordinary scientific prose. A computational pipeline has to make the
disambiguation explicit.

1.3      A typed model of identity
A portable identifier can be represented as a typed tuple

                                               I =( n , o , a , v , r , b , p ) ,
where:
        n is the namespace, such as HGNC, Ensembl, RefSeq, UniProt, ENCODE, or MANTRA;
        o is the object type, such as gene record, transcript feature, protein entry, assay, guide pair, or
         model coordinate;
        a is the accession or local key;
        v is the feature or sequence version when the namespace uses one;
        r is the database release, snapshot, or exact source-file identity;
        b is the genome assembly when genomic coordinates are involved; and
        p is provenance describing the file, resolver, or procedure that asserted the identity.
The tuple is intentionally broader than the visible identifier string. ENST00000611639.4 contains the
Ensembl transcript accession and feature version, while a reproducible interpretation also records the
Ensembl or GENCODE release and assembly. HGNC:33721 is a stable record identifier without a suffix
version, so the snapshot or source-file hash supplies the time-specific context. ALG1L is a meaningful
experimental label when its field is identified as the perturbation row inherited from the library. The
surrounding typed record supplies its namespace, object type, release, and mapping status.

1.4      Orthogonality as a database-design concept
The biological resources discussed in this document are best understood as orthogonal services. In
relational-database terms, they maintain different tables with different primary keys and different
functional dependencies. HGNC is primarily a nomenclature authority. GENCODE is an annotation
release. Ensembl is a genome database with stable feature identifiers. RefSeq is a collection of
accessioned reference sequences. UniProt is a protein knowledgebase. ENCODE is an experimental data
portal. The MANTRA split contract and response axis are project-local ordered coordinate systems.

The overlap among these resources is deliberate. A gene record can have cross-references into several
namespaces because the resources describe complementary properties. The corresponding crosswalks
are usually neither universal bijections nor timeless functions. A gene can have many transcripts. A
transcript may produce a protein or may be noncoding. One gene can have several protein isoforms. A
historical symbol can resolve to a current nomenclature record. A feature can be revised between
annotation releases. A database record can be merged, split, withdrawn, or reclassified. The software
representation should therefore treat cross-resource mappings as release-specific relations.




                              Cross-resource mappings have explicit cardinality and release context.

      Concept box - A symbol is a label, an accession is a record key, and a coordinate is an axis

      These three ideas frequently appear in the same table. A symbol such as TP53 is optimized for human

2      Biological Foundations
2.1    DNA as a physical molecule and an information-bearing polymer
Deoxyribonucleic acid, or DNA, is a polymer whose monomers contain one of four bases: adenine,
cytosine, guanine, and thymine. The bases are conventionally represented by A, C, G, and T. Two
antiparallel DNA strands form a double helix through complementary base pairing: adenine pairs with
thymine, and cytosine pairs with guanine. The order of bases along a strand encodes sequence
information.

A DNA strand has a chemical direction. Its ends are called 5-prime and 3-prime, written 5′ and 3′, after
carbon positions in the deoxyribose sugar. Polymerases synthesize nucleic acids in the 5′ to 3′ direction.
The direction matters whenever a transcript, guide RNA, promoter, coding sequence, or genomic interval
is described. A gene located on the reverse genomic strand is read in the opposite genomic direction from
a gene on the forward strand, even though both RNA products are synthesized 5′ to 3′.

Human nuclear DNA is organized into chromosomes. Most somatic human cells are diploid: they carry
two homologous copies of each autosome, one inherited from each parent, along with a pair of sex
chromosomes. The two homologous chromosomes are similar rather than byte-identical. They can
contain single-nucleotide variants, insertions, deletions, copy-number differences, and larger structural
differences. A reference genome is therefore a coordinate scaffold and representative sequence, rather
than the complete genome sequence of every individual cell.

DNA is packaged with proteins into chromatin. Nucleosomes wrap DNA around histone proteins, and
higher-order chromatin organization affects whether regulatory machinery can access a region. This
physical packaging is central to transcription and explains why chromatin measurements from projects
such as ENCODE can provide useful priors for perturbation modeling.

2.2    Genome, assembly, chromosome, contig, and coordinate
The genome is the complete genetic material of an organism or cell. A genome assembly is a
computationally and manually curated representation of that genome built from sequenced fragments.
An assembly contains chromosome sequences, unplaced or unlocalized contigs, patch sequences, and
metadata. GRCh38 is the thirty-eighth major human reference assembly produced through the Genome
Reference Consortium lineage. The suffix in GRCh38.p14 denotes patch release 14.

A genomic interval is usually represented by chromosome, start, end, and strand. Those values are
meaningful in an assembly-specific coordinate system. The same biological locus can have different
numerical coordinates on GRCh37 and GRCh38. Coordinate-bearing resources therefore carry an
assembly identity. Annotation files and chromatin-signal files are combined through their common
assembly and coordinate conventions.

The coordinate system also has an indexing convention. Some formats use one-based inclusive intervals;
others use zero-based half-open intervals. GTF files use one-based inclusive coordinates. BED files
conventionally use zero-based half-open coordinates. The assembly and interval convention together
determine how numerical coordinates are interpreted.

2.3    The historical and modern meanings of a gene
The gene began as an abstract unit of inheritance. Classical genetics inferred genes from patterns of trait
transmission before the molecular substrate was known. Molecular biology later associated genes with
DNA sequences that encode RNA and, for many genes, proteins. Modern genomics has made the
definition more relational. A gene is an annotated locus associated with one or more RNA products or
other recognized functional outputs, together with regulatory and historical context.

The simple slogan “one gene, one protein” is useful as history but inadequate as a data model. One gene
can have multiple promoters, multiple transcription start sites, alternative splice forms, alternative 3′
ends, and several protein isoforms. Many genes produce functional RNAs without producing proteins.
Some annotated loci are pseudogenes whose evolutionary history connects them to protein-coding genes
while their present coding capacity differs. Some readthrough transcripts span neighboring genes. Some
regulatory effects arise from the act of transcription rather than from a stable RNA product.

For identifier engineering, a gene is best treated as a locus-level biological entity represented by one or
more database records. The gene record can carry a symbol, name, genomic location, locus type, cross-
references, and relations to transcript records. The transcript records then represent the alternative RNA
structures produced from that locus.

2.4    Locus boundaries, promoters, enhancers, and regulatory regions
A gene locus occupies a genomic region, but its biologically relevant regulatory architecture extends
beyond the bases incorporated into mature transcripts. A promoter is a region near a transcription start
site where transcription factors and RNA polymerase assemble. A gene can have several promoters, each
initiating a different family of transcripts. Enhancers are regulatory elements that can increase
transcription from a promoter, often across substantial genomic distances. Silencers, insulators, and
other cis-regulatory elements contribute additional control.

The word cis means that a regulatory element acts through its position on the same DNA molecule or
chromosome context. The word trans refers to diffusible factors, such as transcription-factor proteins or
regulatory RNAs, that can act on other loci. These concepts matter for perturbation labels. A guide can
target a promoter, a distal enhancer, an exon, a splice site, or an entire gene-level intervention. The
intended biological object is established by the library design rather than inferred from a gene symbol
alone.

2.5    Transcription: synthesis of RNA from a DNA template
Transcription is the synthesis of RNA using DNA as a template. RNA polymerase and associated
transcription factors assemble near a promoter, open a local DNA region, and synthesize an RNA strand
complementary to the template strand. The process is conventionally divided into initiation, elongation,
and termination.

For many human protein-coding genes, the first RNA product is a primary transcript or pre-mRNA. The
primary transcript can contain exons, introns, untranslated regions, and a protein-coding region. It is
processed before becoming a mature messenger RNA. Processing commonly includes addition of a 5′ cap,
cleavage and polyadenylation at the 3′ end, and splicing.

An exon is a segment represented in the mature transcript after splicing. An intron is a transcribed
segment removed during splicing. Exons can contain protein-coding sequence, untranslated sequence, or

both. The first exon of a transcript can be entirely untranslated. The last exon can include the end of the
coding sequence and a long 3′ untranslated region. Consequently, “exon” and “protein-coding segment”
are different concepts.

2.6    RNA processing and alternative splicing
Splicing removes introns and ligates selected exons. The spliceosome recognizes sequence and structural
signals around splice sites. Alternative splicing allows a locus to produce more than one mature
transcript. Common patterns include exon skipping, alternative 5′ splice sites, alternative 3′ splice sites,
mutually exclusive exons, and intron retention. Alternative promoters and alternative polyadenylation
sites further expand transcript diversity even when the internal splice pattern is unchanged.




                       One genomic locus can produce several transcript structures and protein outcomes.

A transcript isoform is one particular mature RNA structure associated with a gene. The structure is
defined by an ordered set of exonic intervals on a strand, together with transcript boundaries and, when
applicable, a coding sequence. Two transcripts can differ by a single splice junction, a different start site,
a different terminal exon, or a different retained intron. An annotation database assigns each modeled
transcript its own transcript identifier.

The term transcript model emphasizes that an annotation record is a scientific representation inferred
from evidence. The model specifies the transcript’s genomic structure and attributes. The physical RNA
molecules in cells form a population with variable abundance, processing states, and sometimes
additional heterogeneity. The annotation model provides a stable reference object that sequencing reads
can be assigned to and researchers can discuss.

2.6.1 Exon skipping
In exon skipping, one transcript includes an internal exon while another connects the preceding exon
directly to the following exon. The choice changes the mature RNA sequence. When the skipped exon
intersects the coding sequence, the protein can lose a segment or experience a reading-frame change.

2.6.2 Alternative splice sites
A splice donor or acceptor can be selected at more than one nearby position. The resulting transcripts
differ by a portion of an exon. Small boundary changes can add or remove amino acids, alter a protein
domain, change an untranslated region, or introduce a premature stop codon.

2.6.3 Intron retention
An intron retained in the mature RNA becomes part of the transcript sequence. Retention can regulate
transcript localization, stability, or translation. It can also produce a nonproductive transcript that is
degraded through quality-control pathways.

2.6.4 Alternative transcript starts and ends
A gene can initiate transcription from several promoters and terminate at several polyadenylation sites.
These choices can change the first exon, 5′ untranslated region, coding start, last exon, or 3′ untranslated
region. Alternative ends are part of transcript identity even when most internal exons are shared.

2.7 Isoforms: a precise distinction among transcript, protein, and modification
states
The word isoform is widely used and can hide several distinct mechanisms. A transcript isoform is an
RNA structure. A protein isoform is a distinct protein sequence or proteoform associated with the same
gene. Transcript differences can produce protein differences, with a relation determined by coding
sequence, reading frame, translation, and downstream processing.

A gene can have several transcript isoforms that encode the same amino-acid sequence because their
differences occur only in untranslated regions. A transcript can be annotated as noncoding and therefore
produce no protein. Two coding transcripts can use different start codons or reading frames. A single
protein sequence can also undergo post-translational modifications such as phosphorylation,
glycosylation, acetylation, or proteolytic cleavage. Those modified molecular forms are often called
proteoforms and are conceptually downstream of the transcript-derived protein sequence.

2.8      Translation: from coding RNA to protein
Translation is the synthesis of a polypeptide by the ribosome using a messenger RNA as the template. The
ribosome reads nucleotide triplets called codons. Most codons specify amino acids; stop codons terminate
translation. The coding sequence begins at a selected start codon and ends at a stop codon. The
untranslated regions on either side can influence RNA stability, localization, and translation efficiency
while remaining outside the amino-acid sequence.

A protein is a polymer of amino acids that folds into a three-dimensional structure and can participate in
catalysis, signaling, transport, structural support, regulation, and many other functions. Protein
databases integrate sequence, function, domains, subcellular localization, interactions, disease
associations, and evidence. This is why a UniProt accession is a protein identity rather than a gene
identity.

The mapping from transcript to protein is partial. Protein-coding transcripts can have translated
products. Noncoding transcripts have no ordinary protein product. Some transcript models are retained
as nonproductive or subject to nonsense-mediated decay. Pseudogene transcripts can occasionally have
translation evidence, but that evidence is annotated separately from the locus’s gene classification.

2.9      Coding and noncoding as annotation properties
A protein-coding gene is a locus with at least one transcript whose evidence and sequence support a
protein product. A noncoding RNA gene is primarily annotated for an RNA product rather than a
conventional protein product. The word noncoding describes coding potential in the annotation, while
biological activity can include RNA structure, regulation, localization, and molecular interactions.
Noncoding RNAs can contribute to ribosome structure, RNA processing, chromatin regulation,
transcriptional control, RNA stability, translation, and many other processes.

The coding status can differ at the transcript level within a gene. One gene record may contain coding
transcripts and noncoding transcripts. A transcript can lose coding potential because an alternative splice
pattern disrupts the open reading frame or introduces a premature termination codon. Coding status is
therefore attached to specific transcript models as well as summarized at the gene level.

2.11 Long noncoding RNAs in depth
Long noncoding RNA, abbreviated lncRNA, is an operational category. The conventional boundary of
roughly 200 nucleotides arose partly from laboratory fractionation practices that separated longer RNAs
from well-known small RNAs. It is an operational convention rather than a mechanistic boundary. The
category contains heterogeneous transcripts with different genomic positions, structures, evolutionary
conservation, expression patterns, and functions [18].

A lncRNA can be intergenic, meaning its locus lies between annotated protein-coding genes; antisense,
meaning it overlaps another gene on the opposite strand; intronic, meaning it lies within an intron of
another gene; or bidirectional, meaning its transcription initiates near another promoter in the opposite
direction. GENCODE and HGNC use controlled biotypes and locus types to encode these relationships
[2,6].

Several mechanisms can make a lncRNA locus biologically consequential:
        The RNA molecule can bind proteins, DNA, or other RNAs and act as a scaffold, guide, decoy, or
         regulatory complex component.
        Transcription through the locus can alter local chromatin or interfere with nearby transcription.
        Splicing or promoter activity can influence neighboring regulatory architecture.
        The RNA can modulate stability, localization, or translation of other transcripts.
        Some loci classified as lncRNA may contain short or noncanonical open reading frames, while the
         primary annotation remains noncoding.

These possibilities explain why a gene-level perturbation of a lncRNA locus needs experimental context.
CRISPR interference near the promoter can suppress the act of transcription and the RNA product
together. An RNA-targeting perturbation can affect the RNA molecule more directly. A nuclease-induced
indel inside a noncoding exon may have little effect unless it disrupts an important motif. The
perturbation identifier alone therefore benefits from method and target-region metadata.

2.11.1 lincRNA, lncRNA, and related labels
The term lincRNA means long intergenic noncoding RNA and describes a genomic-context subclass. The
broader term lncRNA includes intergenic, antisense, intronic, and other long noncoding transcripts.
Database biotype vocabularies have changed over time, and a current release may use a more
consolidated terminology than an older release. The exact biotype string should be read in the context of
the annotation release that issued it.

2.11.2 Expression and evidence
Many lncRNAs are expressed at lower abundance or in narrower cell-state ranges than widely expressed
protein-coding genes. Short-read data can provide splice-junction and expression evidence, while long-
read sequencing can reveal full-length structures. Low abundance, repetitive sequence, overlapping
transcription, and incomplete tissue sampling make lncRNA annotation an active area of refinement. This
is one reason release pinning is central to transcript-level identity.

2.12 cDNA: complementary DNA as an experimental representation of RNA
Complementary DNA, abbreviated cDNA, is DNA synthesized from an RNA template by reverse
transcriptase. The “complementary” name reflects base pairing: the DNA sequence synthesized by the
enzyme is complementary to the RNA template. The process is called reverse transcription because
information flows from RNA into DNA, opposite the direction of ordinary cellular transcription [20].

A cDNA molecule represents the RNA that served as its template. When the template is a mature spliced
messenger RNA, the cDNA usually joins the exon-derived sequence and lacks the genomic introns that
were removed from the RNA. This property historically made cDNA clones powerful evidence for
transcript structures. A genomic DNA sequence contains the introns and intergenic context; a mature-
RNA-derived cDNA contains the sequence of the processed RNA captured by the experiment.

The term cDNA is used at several scales:
      A full-length cDNA can represent nearly the complete mature transcript.
      A partial cDNA can cover only part of a transcript.
      A cDNA library is a collection of cDNA molecules made from an RNA population.
      In single-cell RNA sequencing, barcoded cDNA molecules are the amplifiable intermediate from
       which sequencing libraries are built.
      In reference-sequence contexts, “coding DNA” can also appear in variant nomenclature, where it
       refers to coordinates on a coding transcript reference. That use is related but distinct from a
       laboratory cDNA molecule.

RNA is chemically less stable than DNA, while ordinary DNA polymerases amplify DNA templates.
Reverse transcription converts captured RNA molecules into DNA, which can then be amplified and
sequenced. Single-cell workflows add cell barcodes and unique molecular identifiers during or around
first-strand cDNA synthesis so that sequenced reads retain information about the cell and original
molecule from which they arose [16,17].

2.13 Pseudogenes in depth
A pseudogene is a gene-like genomic locus with substantial homology to a functional ancestral gene and a
present classification that differs from an ordinary protein-coding gene. HGNC describes pseudogenes in

                                                            17
                          GENE, TRANSCRIPT, PROTEIN, AND PERTURBATION IDENTIFIER SYSTEMS



terms of homology and inability to produce the ancestral functional protein product, while GENCODE
assigns detailed biotypes reflecting evolutionary origin, transcription, and other evidence [6,19].

Pseudogenes arise through several evolutionary routes.

2.13.1 Duplicated or unprocessed pseudogenes
A genomic segment containing a functional gene can be duplicated. One copy continues to experience
selection for the original function, while the other copy can accumulate substitutions, insertions,
deletions, frameshifts, or premature stop codons. Because the duplication copied genomic DNA, the
pseudogene can retain an exon-intron organization resembling the parent gene. This class is often called
duplicated or unprocessed.

2.13.2 Processed pseudogenes
A mature messenger RNA can be reverse-transcribed into cDNA and inserted into the genome through
retrotransposition. The inserted sequence reflects the processed RNA, so it usually lacks the parent gene’s
introns and can retain signs of a poly(A)-derived tail. The insertion initially lacks the original promoter
and regulatory context, although it may acquire transcriptional activity from its genomic neighborhood.
This class is called processed or retrotransposed.

2.13.3 Unitary pseudogenes
A unitary pseudogene arises when the original functional gene in one lineage accumulates disabling
changes without a retained functional duplicate in that same lineage. A functional ortholog in another
species provides evidence that the ancestral locus was protein-coding. The human locus is then classified
as unitary when the human lineage carries the disrupted form.




                                                       18
                         GENE, TRANSCRIPT, PROTEIN, AND PERTURBATION IDENTIFIER SYSTEMS




                           Pseudogene classes encode evolutionary origin and present structure.

2.13.4 Transcribed and translated pseudogene evidence
Pseudogene status and transcriptional activity are separate properties. Some pseudogene loci are
transcribed. Their RNAs can be by-products of local transcription, or they can have regulatory effects.
GENCODE includes biotypes such as transcribed processed pseudogene and transcribed unprocessed
pseudogene. Some loci have mass-spectrometry or other evidence suggestive of translation, which can be
annotated separately [6].

This separation matters for computational priors. A locus classified as a pseudogene can have an RNA-
expression profile and chromatin context. A protein-sequence prior requires a defensible current protein
product and accession. The typed identity record can represent the locus, transcript evidence, and
protein coverage independently.

2.13.5 Why pseudogene symbols often resemble parent-gene symbols
HGNC pseudogene nomenclature frequently includes a P marker or a parent-derived name. A processed
pseudogene can receive a parent symbol plus P and a number. An unprocessed family-member
pseudogene can carry a family-style symbol ending in P. The spelling communicates lineage and family
relationship, while the stable HGNC ID identifies the nomenclature record [1,19].

2.13.6 Pseudogenes in expression data
Pseudogenes can be difficult to quantify because their sequences may be highly similar to parent genes or
paralogs. Short reads can map ambiguously. An expression matrix can include a pseudogene column if

                         GENE, TRANSCRIPT, PROTEIN, AND PERTURBATION IDENTIFIER SYSTEMS



the annotation and read-assignment procedure support it. The column’s gene symbol, Ensembl ID, and
unique matrix key remain separate pieces of metadata.

2.14 Paralogs, orthologs, and gene families
A gene duplication creates paralogs: related genes within a genome or lineage. Speciation creates
orthologs: genes in different species descended from a gene in their last common ancestor. Gene families
contain homologous genes connected by duplication and divergence. Pseudogenes can also have parent-
gene and family relations.

These relationships motivate another reason symbols alone are insufficient for machine joins. Family
members can have similar symbols and sequences. Cross-species resources can use species-specific
Ensembl prefixes or nomenclature authorities. A human gene identity should therefore carry its species
or taxonomy context alongside the namespace.

3      Genome Annotation
3.1    Assembly and annotation solve different problems
A genome assembly provides the reference sequence and coordinate system. A genome annotation
provides a structured interpretation of that sequence. The assembly answers, “What bases occupy this
reference coordinate?” The annotation answers, “Which biological features are represented at these
coordinates, and how are those features organized into genes, transcripts, exons, coding sequences, and
other records?”

The distinction is foundational. GRCh38.p14 identifies a human assembly state. GENCODE release 44
identifies an annotation package constructed on that assembly and synchronized with Ensembl release
110. A chromatin-signal file can also be aligned to GRCh38. The common assembly permits coordinate
integration, while GENCODE supplies the gene and transcript features used to summarize the signal.

An assembly can remain fixed while annotation releases continue to evolve. New RNA evidence,
improved algorithms, manual curation, and cross-database reconciliation can change the gene set
without changing the underlying chromosome sequence. Conversely, an assembly change can move
coordinates even when the biological interpretation of a gene remains similar.

3.2    What an annotation team actually constructs
An annotation team receives a reference assembly and many forms of evidence. The team or pipeline
identifies candidate transcribed regions, splice junctions, transcript starts and ends, open reading frames,
protein homology, pseudogene signatures, noncoding RNA structures, and other features. It then
produces a coherent, nonredundant set of gene and transcript models.

The resulting annotation is a graph of features rather than a flat list of symbols. A gene feature contains
or is associated with transcript features. Each transcript contains an ordered set of exon features. Coding
transcripts contain coding-sequence segments and translated protein products. Attributes connect
records to HGNC IDs, gene symbols, transcript names, biotypes, support levels, tags, and cross-references.




                                                       21
                         GENE, TRANSCRIPT, PROTEIN, AND PERTURBATION IDENTIFIER SYSTEMS




                      Annotation integrates heterogeneous evidence into a release-specific feature set.


3.3    Sources of annotation evidence
3.3.1 Short-read RNA sequencing
Short-read RNA sequencing produces many fragments derived from RNA molecules. Reads that cross
exon-exon junctions support splice connections. Read coverage supports expression and transcribed
intervals. Short reads are powerful at scale, but reconstructing complete transcript structures from
fragments can be ambiguous when a locus contains many exons and isoforms.

3.3.2 Long-read RNA sequencing
Long-read technologies can sequence much larger portions of individual RNA or cDNA molecules,
sometimes approaching full-length transcripts. They can connect distant splice junctions in a single
observation and thereby resolve combinations of exons. Long reads also have platform-specific error
profiles and sampling limitations, so annotation integrates them with other evidence.

3.3.3 Full-length cDNA and expressed-sequence tags
A full-length cDNA clone provides direct evidence for a processed RNA sequence. Expressed-sequence
tags, historically called ESTs, are shorter cDNA-derived sequence observations. These resources were
especially important before modern high-throughput RNA sequencing and remain part of the evidentiary
history of many transcript records.

3.3.4 Protein evidence
A transcript with an open reading frame can be compared with known proteins, conserved domains, and
mass-spectrometry observations. Protein evidence helps determine coding potential and coding
boundaries. Because protein databases and genome annotations have different objects and release cycles,
the evidence is linked through cross-references rather than represented by one shared identifier.



                                                            22
                           GENE, TRANSCRIPT, PROTEIN, AND PERTURBATION IDENTIFIER SYSTEMS



3.3.5 Comparative genomics
Conservation across species can reveal coding exons, splice sites, regulatory constraints, and ancestral
gene structures. Orthologous protein-coding genes can support the interpretation of a human locus.
Comparative evidence is also important for identifying unitary pseudogenes, where a functional ortholog
exists in another species while the human locus carries disabling changes.

3.3.6 Manual curation and literature
Expert annotators inspect conflicting evidence, published experiments, genomic context, sequence
alignments, and database history. Manual curation is particularly valuable in complex loci, gene families,
readthrough transcripts, pseudogenes, and clinically important genes. GENCODE combines manual
Ensembl-Havana annotation with computational annotation and targeted evidence generation [4].

3.4    Gene models and transcript models
A gene model is the annotation’s representation of a gene locus and its associated transcript set. A
transcript model is one release-specific representation of an RNA structure. The model normally includes:
      chromosome or reference sequence;
      strand;
      transcript start and end;
      ordered exon coordinates;
      splice junctions;
      untranslated regions when defined;
      coding-sequence coordinates when applicable;
      gene and transcript biotypes;
      stable accessions and feature versions;
      cross-references and tags; and
      evidence or support attributes.

The word model denotes an evidence-based representation of a biological RNA class stored by the
database. Physical cells contain individual RNA molecules. The transcript model is the reference object
used to group those molecules and interpret sequencing observations.

3.5    Why transcript annotations change
Transcript models change for several reasons. New evidence can reveal an additional exon, a different
splice boundary, a previously unobserved transcript start, or a longer 3′ end. A sequence error can be
corrected. A transcript can be reassigned to another gene after locus boundaries are revised. Two models
can be merged, or one model can be split. Coding potential can be re-evaluated. A transcript can be
retained while its version increments, or its stable ID can be retired when continuity criteria are no
longer met.

Ensembl’s stable-ID versioning rules encode entity-specific structural changes. Gene versions increment
when the linked transcript set changes. Transcript versions increment for changes in splicing pattern,
chromosomal location, or cDNA sequence. Protein versions increment when the peptide sequence
changes. Exon versions increment when the exon genomic sequence changes [3].

ENST00000611639 identifies the transcript record lineage; .4 identifies the fourth feature version under

that version is interpreted.

3.6      Canonical, representative, principal, and MANE transcripts
Many applications require one transcript per gene. Databases therefore provide representative
selections. The policy varies by resource and use case. A canonical transcript can be chosen using coding
conservation, expression, transcript support, protein evidence, clinical relevance, or other criteria. The
choice is a projection from a one-to-many gene-to-transcript relation into a single default record.

MANE, the Matched Annotation from NCBI and EMBL-EBI project, seeks exact agreement between
selected RefSeq and Ensembl/GENCODE transcripts for human protein-coding genes. A MANE Select pair
gives a matched transcript whose exon structure and sequence agree across the two annotation
ecosystems. MANE Plus Clinical provides additional transcripts needed to represent clinically relevant
variants. These resources improve interoperability while preserving the separate RefSeq and Ensembl
namespaces.

CCDS, the Consensus Coding Sequence project, similarly focuses on agreed protein-coding regions across
collaborating resources. CCDS IDs describe consensus coding sequences rather than all transcript
features. MANE, CCDS, canonical-transcript tags, and principal-isoform selections are related but distinct
constructs.
      Concept box - Representative transcript selection is a function of purpose.
      A transcript chosen for clinical variant reporting can differ from a transcript chosen for broad
      expression analysis or protein-domain coverage. A data pipeline should record the selection policy and
      selected accession rather than assume that the word canonical has one global meaning.

3.8    Gene and transcript biotypes
A biotype is a controlled annotation category. Gene-level and transcript-level biotypes can differ. A
protein-coding gene can contain a retained-intron transcript or another transcript whose coding potential
differs from the gene’s primary class. Pseudogene biotypes can encode origin and transcriptional
evidence. Noncoding loci can be subdivided by genomic context or RNA class.

Biotype vocabularies evolve. A parser should retain the exact source string and, when needed, map it into
a project-level ontology through a versioned mapping table. This preserves the original annotation while
supporting stable downstream categories.

3.9    Genome assembly patches and alternate loci
A patch release such as GRCh38.p14 adds corrections or alternate representations without redefining
every primary-chromosome coordinate. Fix patches provide corrected sequence intended for future
incorporation. Novel patches provide alternate representations of structurally variable regions.
Assemblies can also include alternate loci, decoy sequences, and unplaced contigs.

The presence of alternate representations means that “the gene’s coordinate” can depend on which
assembly component is selected. Most expression pipelines operate on the primary assembly or a defined
reference bundle. The reference bundle, aligner index, annotation file, and feature-counting
configuration together establish the coordinate universe used to build the matrix.

3.10 Coordinate transformations and lift-over
Lift-over maps coordinates between assemblies using alignment chains or related mappings. The
transformation can be partial or ambiguous. A region can fail to map, map to several locations, change
length, or cross a rearranged interval. Lift-over is therefore a provenance-bearing operation rather than
a textual replacement of the assembly label.

For gene-level aggregation, the preferred approach is usually to interpret each source in its native pinned
assembly and annotation, then map through stable gene or transcript identities when those mappings are
available. Coordinate lift-over remains valuable for signal tracks and intervals, provided the
transformation procedure and result quality are recorded.

3.11 Release pinning as scientific state management
A database release is analogous to a versioned state of a large knowledge graph. It contains many records
and relations that were mutually consistent under the project’s policies at publication time.
Reproducibility requires more than an accession string when the accession’s attributes and cross-
references can evolve.

Within a release, one Ensembl gene usually contains one or more Ensembl transcripts. A coding

join therefore traverses the release graph rather than substituting prefixes in an accession string.

4.4.2 Species context
Ensembl prefixes can include species codes. Human stable IDs have the familiar ENSG, ENST, and ENSP
forms. Mouse identifiers include ENSMUSG, ENSMUST, and ENSMUSP. A robust parser treats prefix structure as a
type hint and retains the species or taxonomy context as an explicit field.

5       Perturbation Experiments and Guide Architecture
5.1     Perturbation as an experimental object
A perturbation is an intervention applied to a biological system in order to alter a molecular process and
observe the resulting phenotype. Perturbations can be genetic, chemical, environmental, or physical. In
the present data stack, the central perturbations are CRISPR-based genetic interventions whose target
labels are associated with genes and transcripts.

The experimental identity is more specific than the biological target name. “Perturb TP53” can refer to a
nuclease knockout, promoter repression by CRISPR interference, promoter activation by CRISPR
activation, transcript degradation, base editing, or a pair of guides designed to delete a region. Each
method acts through a different mechanism and can produce a different distribution of molecular
outcomes. The perturbation record therefore includes the target label, guide or guide pair, construct
architecture, effector system, and library context.

5.2     CRISPR-Cas targeting from first principles
CRISPR systems use a guide RNA to direct a Cas protein or Cas-derived effector to a DNA sequence
through base pairing. A commonly used single-guide RNA, or sgRNA, contains a variable targeting region
and a constant scaffold region. The targeting region is designed to be complementary to a genomic
sequence adjacent to a protospacer-adjacent motif, abbreviated PAM, recognized by the Cas protein.

The guide RNA and Cas protein form a complex. The guide targeting sequence base-pairs with genomic
DNA at compatible sites. The PAM and guide-target complementarity determine candidate binding. The
downstream biological effect depends on the effector:
       Active Cas9 can introduce a double-strand break.
       Catalytically inactive Cas9, called dCas9, can bind DNA without cutting it.
       dCas9 fused to transcriptional repressor domains can implement CRISPR interference, or CRISPRi.
       dCas9 fused to activator domains can implement CRISPR activation, or CRISPRa.
       Base editors and prime editors use guide-directed binding to implement other sequence changes.

A guide identity is consequently an experimental sequence and construct record. Its gene assignment is
derived from guide sequence, reference assembly, target region, and library design. Gene targeting is an
annotation relation derived from the guide sequence, reference assembly, target region, and library
design.

5.3     CRISPR nuclease knockout
In a conventional Cas9 knockout experiment, the guide directs nuclease-active Cas9 to a genomic site.
Repair of the double-strand break can introduce insertions or deletions. When the target lies in a coding
exon, some repair outcomes disrupt the reading frame or important protein sequence. The population
can contain several alleles and editing outcomes.

The intended perturbation label is often a gene, while the physical target is a genomic protospacer. The
outcome depends on guide efficiency, repair distribution, ploidy, copy number, and whether all
functional alleles are disrupted. A gene-level label summarizes the intervention intent; the guide and
target coordinates describe the actual design.



                                                       35
                          GENE, TRANSCRIPT, PROTEIN, AND PERTURBATION IDENTIFIER SYSTEMS



5.4      CRISPR interference
CRISPRi uses catalytically inactive Cas9 fused to a transcriptional repressor, such as a KRAB-containing
effector. The sgRNA directs the complex to a promoter or transcription-start-site region. The bound
effector recruits repressive chromatin machinery and reduces transcription. CRISPRi therefore produces
knockdown rather than a DNA-cutting genotype.

The mechanism has several consequences for identity. The target region is often promoter-specific. A
gene with several promoters can have promoter-dependent effects. A transcript label in a library can
encode the promoter or transcript context used during guide design. A historical transcript assignment
can remain important experimental metadata even when a newer annotation release reorganizes the
locus.

The Replogle genome-scale Perturb-seq experiments used CRISPRi to target expressed genes across
millions of human cells [13]. The associated dual-sgRNA library work showed that two guides targeting
the same gene can increase knockdown while allowing a compact library architecture [14,15].

5.5      CRISPR activation
CRISPRa uses dCas9 fused or recruited to transcriptional activators. Guides are designed near promoters
or other regulatory regions to increase expression. The perturbation label can again be gene-level while
the physical target is a promoter-region sequence. A guide pair can increase activation efficacy or target
alternative promoters.

5.7    Single-guide library elements
A single-guide library element encodes one sgRNA. The element can be delivered to cells with a lentiviral
vector or another system. In a pooled screen, many cells receive different library elements. The
abundance or phenotype of cells associated with each guide is then measured.

A gene can be represented by several independent single-guide elements. Multiple guides increase the
chance that at least one guide has strong activity and allow agreement across guides to support the gene-
level effect. The cost is a larger library and a larger number of cells required to maintain coverage.

For single-cell screens, the guide identity can be captured directly, inferred through a linked guide
barcode, or assigned through another indexing strategy. The cell-level record associates one or more
guide identities with the cell barcode.

5.8    Dual-guide elements targeting one gene
A dual-guide element encodes two sgRNAs, commonly called guide A and guide B, in one construct. When
both guides target the same gene, they bind two distinct target sites associated with the gene’s promoter
or regulatory region. The two guide activities can combine to produce stronger or more consistent
transcriptional repression than one guide alone.




                       Single-guide and paired-guide constructs support several experimental designs.

The central distinction is between two guides per construct and two genes per perturbation. A same-gene
dual-guide element contains two sgRNAs whose intended target gene is the same. The biological
perturbation remains one gene-level intervention. The exact construct identity retains both guide
components.

The Replogle dual-sgRNA CRISPRi design selected pairs of high-performing guides for each gene and
expressed them from a tandem cassette. The design enabled one compact library element to carry two
targeting sequences. In direct-capture Perturb-seq, both guide positions can be sequenced and assigned at
the single-cell level [14,15].

5.8.1 Why two guides can improve knockdown
Guide efficacy varies because of target accessibility, sequence context, distance from the transcription
start site, chromatin state, guide expression, and effector occupancy. Two independently selected high-
activity guides provide two binding opportunities. Their combined repression can increase the
probability and magnitude of target knockdown across cells.

The pair also changes how activity is summarized. A dual-guide construct is a single experimental
element with a combined phenotype. The individual guide scores remain useful metadata, while the
measured response belongs to the pair as delivered.

5.8.2 Guide A and guide B as positional identities
The A/B labels usually denote positions in the vector cassette. Position can matter for expression
promoter, constant region, guide capture, amplification, or recombination analysis. A pair should
therefore be represented as an ordered structure when the vector architecture is ordered:

                                                q= ( q A , q B , v , c ) ,

where q A and q B are guide identities, v is the vector or cassette design, and c is the construct identity.
Even when the biological targeting effect is approximately symmetric, the experimental record preserves
the positions.

5.9     Paired guides targeting different genes
A dual-guide construct can also target two different genes. This design supports combinatorial
perturbation and genetic-interaction mapping. The pair geneA + geneB is then a distinct perturbation
from either single-gene intervention. Controls often pair a targeting guide with a non-targeting guide so
that single-gene effects can be estimated under the same vector architecture.

In a genetic-interaction design, several guide sequences for gene A can be combined with several guide
sequences for gene B. The construct-level space is the Cartesian product of selected guide sets. Gene-pair
effects are aggregated across constructs while retaining guide-level measurements and positional
controls.

This use of paired guides should be distinguished from the same-gene dual-guide architecture in the K562
atlas. The field name sgID_AB communicates that the construct contains A and B guide identities; the
target metadata determines whether the pair represents two guides for one gene, two genes, a flanking
deletion, or another design.

5.10 Paired guides for genomic deletions
Nuclease-active Cas9 can use two guides flanking a genomic region. Cutting at both sites can delete the
intervening DNA. This architecture is common for noncoding regulatory elements, exons, or larger
segments. Here, the guide pair defines two boundary coordinates, and the intended perturbation object is
the interval between them.

5.12 Direct guide capture
Direct-capture Perturb-seq modifies the guide RNA architecture so that guide-derived sequences can be
captured and sequenced alongside the single-cell transcriptome. The guide assignment becomes an
observed molecular measurement rather than an inference from a separate linked barcode. Direct
capture supports cells containing multiple guides and makes combinatorial or dual-guide designs easier
to resolve [15].

At a high level, the output contains two linked measurement streams:
  1.   gene-expression cDNA reads carrying cell barcodes and UMIs; and
  2.   guide-derived reads carrying the same cell-barcode context.

The cell barcode joins the streams. The guide assignment establishes the experimental intervention, and
the gene-expression matrix establishes the cellular response.

5.13 Linked barcodes and direct capture
Earlier Perturb-seq implementations often associated each guide with a separate guide barcode
transcribed in a polyadenylated transcript. The barcode was captured by standard RNA-seq chemistry
and mapped back to the guide. Direct capture sequences the expressed guide more directly. Both
strategies create an intervention-to-cell relation, but their molecular objects and error modes differ.

A data contract should state which field contains the guide identity, whether the identity was directly
captured or inferred, how multiple guides were called, and how uncertain assignments were
represented.

5.14 Lentiviral delivery and recombination context
Dual-guide constructs are frequently delivered with lentiviral vectors. Lentiviral particles package two
RNA genomes, and reverse transcription during integration can switch templates. In pooled dual-guide
libraries, template switching can create recombined A/B pairs. Modern designs and sequencing strategies
quantify the observed pair and can exclude or separately analyze recombined constructs. Replogle and
colleagues reported direct sequencing of both positions and explicit recombination handling in dual-
sgRNA screens [14].

For identifier design, the important principle is that the observed construct identity can differ from the
planned library row. The raw guide-pair call should preserve the observed A and B identities. A planned-
construct ID and a recombination or validity flag can then describe the relationship to the design table.

5.15 Multiplicity of infection, singlets, and multiple guide assignments
Multiplicity of infection, abbreviated MOI, describes the average number of viral integration events per
cell. Low-MOI designs aim to place one library element in most infected cells, which simplifies causal
attribution. Some screens intentionally use higher multiplicity or combinatorial constructs. Droplet-based
single-cell sequencing can also capture more than one cell in a droplet, creating a multiplet.

A cell can therefore have several guide-derived sequences for several reasons: a designed dual-guide
cassette, multiple viral integrations, a recombined construct, ambient guide molecules, or a cell multiplet.
Guide-calling software distinguishes these possibilities using sequence counts, expected architecture,
quality thresholds, and cell-level metadata. The final cell record should preserve the called construct,
component guides, assignment confidence, and multiplicity flags when available.

5.16 Target identity versus response identity
The perturbation target and the measured response genes live on different axes. A guide pair targeting
FNTB produces a transcriptional response measured over thousands of genes. The target identity indexes
the intervention row. The response-gene identifiers index the expression vector. This asymmetry is the
biological basis for MANTRA’s separate perturbation and response axes.

5.17 Controls
Non-targeting controls use guide sequences designed to lack a relevant genomic target under the library’s
criteria. They estimate the baseline effects of vector delivery, guide expression, effector activity, selection,
and assay handling. Positive controls target genes with expected phenotypes. Targeting-plus-control pairs
can calibrate one side of a paired architecture.

The label non-targeting is an experimental category. It can occupy fields that otherwise carry symbols or
gene IDs, but it represents a control intervention rather than a biological gene record. A typed schema
assigns it an experimental-control object type.

6       Single-Cell Perturbation Data
6.1     What Perturb-seq measures
Perturb-seq combines pooled CRISPR perturbations with single-cell RNA sequencing. Each cell has an
intervention identity and a transcriptional phenotype. The central output can be viewed as a set of tuples

                                                          ( c i , qi , x i ) ,
where c i is a cell identity, q i is the called perturbation or guide construct, and x i is a vector of expression
measurements across annotated genes.

The power of the assay comes from the high-dimensional phenotype. Instead of measuring one survival
score or reporter, the experiment observes the state of thousands of genes in each cell. Aggregating cells
by perturbation yields pseudobulk response profiles, while retaining cells supports heterogeneity, state,
trajectory, and distributional analyses.




                            Perturb-seq connects guide identity to a single-cell expression phenotype.


6.2     From cellular RNA to sequencing reads
A droplet-based single-cell RNA-seq workflow begins with a suspension of cells. Microfluidic partitioning
places individual cells together with barcoded gel beads and reaction reagents in droplets or emulsions.
Messenger RNAs with poly(A) tails hybridize to oligo-dT primers. Reverse transcriptase synthesizes cDNA
and incorporates barcode sequences.

A typical molecule carries several information components:
       a sample index identifying the sequencing library or sample pool;
       a cell barcode identifying the droplet or cell partition;
       a unique molecular identifier, or UMI, labeling the original captured molecule;


                                                                 42
                          GENE, TRANSCRIPT, PROTEIN, AND PERTURBATION IDENTIFIER SYSTEMS



      primer and adapter sequences required for amplification and sequencing; and
      a cDNA insert derived from the captured RNA.

The exact architecture varies by chemistry. In 10x Genomics 3′ gene-expression chemistry, cDNA
molecules generated in one gel-bead emulsion share a cell barcode, and UMIs distinguish original
captured transcript molecules within that cell [16,17].

6.3    Cell barcodes
A cell barcode is a sequence drawn from a designed whitelist. Molecules synthesized in one droplet
receive the same barcode. After sequencing, reads with the same valid cell barcode are assigned to the
same putative cell.

A barcode is an assay-level identifier whose uniqueness is scoped to a library or run. Its uniqueness is
scoped to the library or run. A complete cell key commonly includes sample or library identity plus the
cell barcode. Concatenating those fields or using an explicit composite key prevents collisions across files.

6.4    Unique molecular identifiers
A UMI is a short random sequence attached to an original captured molecule. Multiple sequencing reads
can originate from PCR copies of the same cDNA. Reads sharing cell barcode, gene assignment, and UMI
are collapsed to estimate one original molecule. This reduces amplification bias.

UMIs are local molecular tags. Collisions can occur when two original molecules receive the same UMI
within the same cell and gene context. Error-correction procedures also merge nearby UMI sequences
under defined rules. The resulting matrix value is therefore an estimated molecule count under the
pipeline’s counting model.

6.5    Read alignment and gene assignment
The cDNA-derived read is aligned or pseudoaligned to a reference genome or transcriptome. The pipeline
uses an annotation to assign compatible reads to genes. Reads overlapping exons, splice junctions,
introns, or multiple loci can be treated differently depending on the software and configuration.

The matrix gene axis inherits the identifiers used by the reference bundle. A Cell Ranger reference, for
example, is constructed from a FASTA assembly and GTF annotation. The feature table can use Ensembl
gene IDs as primary feature IDs and gene symbols as display names. The resulting matrix therefore
reflects both the biological sample and the reference annotation.

6.6    Counts, normalization, and transformed expression
Raw UMI counts are nonnegative integers. Downstream analysis can normalize each cell by library size,
scale to a target total, apply log1p, select variable genes, or construct residual representations. The
identifier axis remains conceptually independent of the numerical transformation.

The MANTRA raw atlas contract records .X as log1p CP10K expression for the pinned slim atlas. This
semantic statement is part of the file’s data contract. A matrix with the same shape but raw counts would

6.7      Pseudobulk aggregation
A pseudobulk profile aggregates single-cell expression values over a group, often all cells sharing a
perturbation. For raw counts, aggregation can sum molecules before normalization. For transformed
values, the chosen averaging and baseline procedure determine the resulting statistic. The perturbation
identity defines the grouping axis; the gene identifiers define the vector coordinates.

Pseudobulk aggregation retains cell-level provenance as part of the aggregate definition. Quality-control
masks, control matching, guide assignments, cell states, and batch variables determine which cells
contribute to each aggregate.

6.9      The two dimensions of an expression matrix
An expression matrix has a row identity and a column identity. In the atlas, rows are cells. Columns are
expression-gene features. A perturbation label is row metadata rather than a column key. The same gene
name can therefore appear in two roles:
        as the intended perturbation attached to a cell; and
        as one coordinate in the expression vector measured for that cell.

These roles are causally connected but structurally independent. A cell perturbed at gene A has
measurements for gene A, gene B, gene C, and thousands of other response genes. The perturbation axis
and expression axis can have different identifier systems, cardinalities, and duplicate-resolution rules.

6.11 Direct guide capture as a second feature space
A direct-capture Perturb-seq experiment can produce a gene-expression library and a guide-capture
library from the same cells. Both libraries contain cell barcodes. The guide reads are counted against a
guide feature reference, while the cDNA reads are counted against a gene annotation. The two feature
spaces are joined through cell barcodes.

The guide feature ID is therefore parallel to a gene feature ID at the assay level, but the biological object
types differ. One is an experimental construct sequence. The other is a gene or transcript annotation
feature. A multimodal container can store both, or a preprocessing pipeline can materialize the called
guide identities in obs.

6.12 Controls and matched-control baselines
Non-targeting cells provide empirical control distributions. MANTRA additionally uses matched-control
procedures to estimate baseline expression for treated cells. The control cell identity, matching
procedure, and residual target representation are downstream modeling objects. They remain keyed by
the same expression-gene response axis.

Every arrow applies an algorithm or experimental transformation. The identifier contract records

7        MANTRA Identifier Architecture
7.1      Architectural goal
The MANTRA identity layer supports a perturbation-response model built from historical experimental
labels, contemporary biological priors, and a separately reconciled response-gene surface. Its goal is to
preserve every relevant identity at the level where it is valid while exposing stable ordered axes to model
code.

The architecture has two canonical model-facing spaces:
    1.   the perturbation axis, whose rows are the exact gene-symbol labels inherited from the fit, tune,
         and hold split contract; and
    2.   the response-gene axis, whose columns are unique resolved_gene_key values constructed by
         reconciling the GEARS surface to atlas genes through Ensembl gene IDs.

These spaces are connected through typed metadata. They remain separate because the intervention row
and predicted gene-expression coordinate are different objects.

7.5      Why the perturbation label remains historical
The guide library was designed under an annotation and nomenclature context available at design time.

added as a resolution layer.

This is analogous to preserving an original foreign key from a source transaction while attaching a
dimension-table key for current analytics. The source identity is immutable provenance. The resolved
identity is a curated interpretation. Both are required for a stable warehouse.

7.7    Construction of the 5,000-gene response axis

7.12 Resolution precedence
The nomenclature resolver applies a deterministic precedence:
  1.     exact approved HGNC symbol match;
  2.     unique previous-symbol match;
  3.     unique alias-symbol match;
  4.     direct stable-ID cross-reference when the source provides one;
  5.     explicit ambiguous or unresolved state.

The procedure preserves the input string and returns the full candidate evidence. A direct source
Ensembl gene ID can support or disambiguate symbol resolution. Transcript membership is evaluated
inside the pinned annotation release.

7.13 Using Ensembl IDs for locus and matrix reconciliation
Ensembl gene IDs serve two related roles. In raw perturbation metadata, obs/gene_id describes the
targeted locus as recorded by the atlas. In expression metadata, var/gene_id describes each measured
gene feature. In the GEARS surface builder, Ensembl IDs reconcile source rows to atlas columns.

The shared namespace enables a join, while field role remains explicit. An Ensembl gene ID in obs is
target metadata. An Ensembl gene ID in var is measurement-axis metadata. A typed schema can use
names such as target_ensembl_gene_id and response_ensembl_gene_id when the fields coexist in one
record.

7.14 Transcript interpretation
Raw transcript labels are preserved exactly because they document library design. Current transcript
membership is computed against GENCODE release 44. The resolver distinguishes the stable transcript
stem from the version and looks up the full accession when available.

A transcript stem can be reassigned or have a different version in a later release. The project records:
      raw transcript string;
      parsed namespace and stable stem;
      raw feature version;
      current release lookup result;
      current gene membership;
      match relation such as exact accession.version, stem-only continuation, historical context, or
       absent in release.

This representation makes release drift visible without rewriting the experiment.

7.15 Protein-derived prior coverage
Protein-derived priors require a locus that supports a current protein product and a resolved protein
accession. The identity row records the chosen UniProt accession and sequence hash. The selection policy
can prioritize reviewed entries, canonical sequences, or a defined isoform set.

For a pseudogene such as current ALG1L1P, an empty current UniProt accession list is a resolved
outcome. Other source databases may contain historical or similarity-based protein references; the
MANTRA record follows the pinned current-locus policy and retains the provenance of any alternate
evidence separately.

7.16 Coordinate-derived prior coverage
Chromatin, regulatory, and genomic-neighborhood priors are computed from coordinate-bearing
sources. Each row records:
      source assay or file accession;
      source assembly;
      annotation release used to define gene intervals;
      aggregation window and strand policy;
      transformation or lift-over metadata;
      exact file hashes; and
      output feature semantics.

This separates measured signal from the gene coordinates used to summarize it. An ENCODE file and a
GENCODE GTF remain independent inputs joined through GRCh38.p14 coordinates.

9.6       Invariants for the K562 atlas contract
The following invariants define the pinned identity surface:
    1.    The raw H5AD has SHA-256 51d6c7ecb349d34ec4c0e1bfb44a6d832e4113b26f46a319fc7993c028a5f060.
    2.    The matrix has 310,385 cell rows and 8,563 expression-gene columns.
    3.    Cell metadata represent 2,057 gene-target perturbations and one non-targeting control label.
    4.    The split contract contains 1,427 fit, 357 tune, and 273 hold perturbation rows.
    5.    The perturbation key vector preserves exact split-contract strings and order.
    6.    Approved, previous-symbol, and alias resolutions record a unique selected HGNC record or an
          explicit candidate state.
    7.    Protein-derived rows record the chosen protein accession and exact sequence identity.
    8.    Coordinate-derived rows record assembly, annotation source, and source-file hash.
    9.    The 5,000 response keys are unique.
    10.   Ensembl reconciliation retains both TBCE coordinates.

These invariants can be evaluated in unit tests and artifact-registration scripts. They transform the
identity layer from descriptive documentation into an executable contract.

9.7       Build reports and manifests
MANTRA separates build provenance from read semantics.

A build report answers:
         Which script wrote the artifact?
         Which command and parameters were used?
         Which input bytes were read?

        What output bytes, shape, dtype, and fields were produced?

A manifest answers:
        What does the artifact represent?

9.10 Diagnostic summaries
Useful diagnostics include:
         counts by resolution status and match kind;
         approved versus previous-symbol versus alias matches;
         one-to-many transcript and protein relation counts;
         locus-type distribution;
         protein-coverage rate by locus type;
         exact versus stem-only transcript-version matches;
         response-axis duplicate symbol counts;
         guide-pair multiplicity per perturbation;
         cells per construct and perturbation;
         source-release and assembly coverage; and
         hashes of every ordered axis.

Diagnostics are most useful when they are emitted as structured tables as well as human-readable
reports. The structured form supports regression tests across rebuilds.

9.11 Versioned policy
Resolution is partly a policy. Examples include the precedence among approved, previous, and alias
symbols; the choice of UniProt reviewed versus unreviewed entries; the handling of transcript stems
whose current membership changed; and the construction of unique response keys.

The policy should have a versioned identifier, such as identifier_resolution.v1. A policy update creates
a new resolver output and comparison report. The source experimental axis can remain fixed while
current metadata are refreshed under a new policy.

9.13 Provenance hashes and content addressing
Cryptographic hashes identify exact file bytes. For compressed files, the hash of the distributed
compressed bytes and the hash of decompressed semantic content can both be useful. Sequence caches
can hash canonical FASTA bytes after a documented normalization. Ordered vectors can hash newline-
delimited UTF-8 keys.

A hash establishes whether two artifacts are byte-identical under the chosen serialization. The manifest
describes semantics. The manifest supplies semantics, and tests connect the two.

9.14 Human-readable documentation and machine-readable contracts
The monograph explains why the architecture exists. Machine-readable schemas enforce it. Every
important table in this document should have a corresponding contract in JSON Schema, Pydantic,
Arrow, YAML, or test code. The prose and schema should share field names and version identifiers.

The recommended documentation stack has three layers:
  1.      this conceptual monograph;
  2.      a concise engineering specification containing schemas, algorithms, and invariants; and
  3.      artifact-local manifests and build reports containing exact resolved values.

Appendix E. Glossary
Accession. A stable identifier assigned to a database record. An accession can have a version suffix when
the namespace versions individual features or sequences.

Alias symbol. A symbol associated with an HGNC record through literature or database usage that is
distinct from the current approved symbol and previous approved symbols.

Annotation. A structured interpretation of a genome assembly that defines gene, transcript, exon,
coding-sequence, and other features.

Assembly. A named reference representation of a genome and its coordinate system.

Barcode. A designed sequence used to associate a sequencing molecule with a cell, sample, guide, or
other experimental unit. Barcode scope is determined by the assay.

Biotype. A controlled category assigned to a gene or transcript feature, such as protein-coding, lncRNA,
retained intron, or processed pseudogene.



                                                       68
                          GENE, TRANSCRIPT, PROTEIN, AND PERTURBATION IDENTIFIER SYSTEMS



Canonical transcript. A transcript selected as a representative under a particular resource’s policy.

cDNA. DNA synthesized from an RNA template by reverse transcriptase. In RNA sequencing it is the
amplifiable DNA representation of captured RNA.

Cell barcode. A sequence identifying the droplet or cell partition from which a set of molecules
originated.

Chromatin. DNA packaged with histones and other proteins. Chromatin state influences regulatory
accessibility.

Coding sequence (CDS). The portion of a transcript translated into a protein sequence.

Contig. A contiguous assembled sequence that can form part of a chromosome or remain unplaced.

Cross-reference. A database assertion connecting a record to a record in another namespace.

CRISPR activation (CRISPRa). Guide-directed recruitment of transcriptional activators to increase
expression.

CRISPR interference (CRISPRi). Guide-directed recruitment of a catalytically inactive Cas effector and
transcriptional repressor to reduce expression.

Display symbol. A human-readable gene label presented in tables and plots. It can have different
uniqueness properties from a database accession or model key.

Dual-guide element. A library construct encoding two sgRNAs, usually represented as guide A and guide
B.

Enhancer. A cis-regulatory genomic element that can increase transcription from a promoter in a
context-dependent manner.

Exon. A segment retained in a mature transcript after RNA splicing. Exons can contain coding and
untranslated sequence.

Feature. An annotated genomic object such as a gene, transcript, exon, or coding-sequence interval.

Feature version. A revision number attached to an individual Ensembl feature record according to
object-specific rules.

Gene. A locus-level biological entity associated with one or more RNA products or other recognized
functional outputs.

Gene model. A release-specific annotation representation of a gene locus and its associated transcript
structures.

Gene symbol. A human-readable short label for a gene, such as an HGNC-approved symbol or a historical
experimental symbol.

Genome. The complete genetic material of an organism or cell.

GTF. A tabular annotation format whose rows encode genomic features, coordinates, and attributes.

Guide RNA. An RNA molecule that directs a CRISPR effector to a sequence through base pairing and PAM-
compatible targeting.



                                                       69
                          GENE, TRANSCRIPT, PROTEIN, AND PERTURBATION IDENTIFIER SYSTEMS



Guide-pair identity. The exact identifier of a construct containing guide A and guide B.

Haplotype. A linked set of sequence variants on one chromosome copy.

HGNC ID. Stable identifier for a human gene-nomenclature record.

Intron. A transcribed segment removed from a primary RNA during splicing.

Isoform. One of several transcript structures or protein sequences associated with a gene. The object
type should be specified.

Lift-over. A coordinate transformation between genome assemblies based on sequence alignments or
mapping chains.

lncRNA. Long noncoding RNA, an operationally defined heterogeneous class of noncoding transcripts
commonly longer than 200 nucleotides.

Locus. A location or interval in the genome associated with a biological or annotation feature.

MANE. A cross-resource matched transcript set in which selected RefSeq and Ensembl/GENCODE
transcript structures and sequences agree.

Manifest. A machine-readable semantic contract describing an artifact’s meaning, fields, axes, and
permitted uses.

Namespace. The issuing identification system and rules under which an accession has meaning.

NCBI GeneID. Stable numeric identifier for an NCBI Gene record.

Noncoding RNA. An RNA whose primary annotated product is the RNA rather than a conventional
protein.

Object type. The class of entity identified, such as gene record, transcript feature, protein entry, assay
file, guide pair, or model coordinate.

Ortholog. A gene in another species related through speciation from a gene in the common ancestor.

Paralog. A related gene produced through duplication within a lineage.

PAM. Protospacer-adjacent motif recognized by a Cas protein near the guide target site.

Perturbagen. The agent or intervention applied to alter a biological system.

Perturbation identity. The project key for an experimental intervention row. In MANTRA it is the exact
split-contract symbol inherited from the atlas.

Poly(A) tail. A run of adenosines added to the 3′ end of many mature eukaryotic RNAs and used for oligo-
dT capture.

Primary transcript. The initial RNA synthesized from DNA before completion of processing such as
splicing.

Promoter. A genomic region where transcriptional machinery assembles to initiate transcription.

Protein isoform. A distinct amino-acid sequence associated with the same gene or protein entry.

Pseudobulk. An aggregate expression profile constructed from a group of single cells.

Pseudogene. A gene-like locus with homology to a functional ancestral gene and a present annotation
reflecting disrupted or altered protein-coding capacity.

Release. A published database-wide state containing a coherent set of records and relations.

Resolved gene key. A unique MANTRA response-axis key constructed for one model coordinate.

Reverse transcription. Synthesis of DNA from an RNA template.

Sequence version. A suffix identifying a revision of an accessioned sequence, as in RefSeq
accession.version.

sgID_AB. The atlas field holding the exact paired guide A/B identity.

Splice junction. The connection between two exons in a processed transcript.

Transcript. A specific RNA structure produced from a locus, represented by an ordered set of exons and
transcript boundaries.

Transcript isoform. One alternative mature RNA structure associated with a gene.

Transcript model. An annotation record representing the inferred structure and attributes of one
transcript class.

UMI. Unique molecular identifier used to distinguish original captured molecules before PCR
amplification.

UniProt accession. Identifier for a UniProt protein knowledgebase entry; an isoform suffix can identify a
represented isoform.

Version. A revision identifier attached to an individual feature or sequence record under namespace-
specific rules.

Appendix H. Final canonical answer
A consistent gene-identification system for the MANTRA perturbation stack is a typed record with several
orthogonal fields. The experimental perturbation row is the exact symbol inherited from the split
contract. The raw atlas supplies target Ensembl gene IDs, transcript labels, and guide-pair identities.
HGNC supplies current human gene nomenclature. Ensembl supplies stable gene and transcript feature
records. GENCODE release 44 on GRCh38.p14 supplies release-specific genomic structure. NCBI Gene and
RefSeq supply gene records and reference sequences. UniProt supplies protein knowledge and exact
sequence identities. ENCODE supplies assay and file provenance. The 5,000-gene model response surface
uses unique resolved_gene_key values reconciled through Ensembl gene IDs.

The complete semantics are preserved by a collection of typed projections and the versioned relations
among them. This collection is the stable computational representation.
